<SpineSection label="Context" subhead="A male-dominated category with a proven primary purchaser">

Sustain made natural condoms, lube, and wipes, then organic tampons, pads, and liners. The sexual wellness aisle was built for men — clinical packaging, bro humor, or both — while women were already doing much of the buying.

Sustain sold to that buyer directly, in a category the FDA doesn't require to disclose ingredients.

</SpineSection>

<SpineSection label="Complication" subhead="Bold without being crude, inclusive without being soft">

The brand had to say *vagina* on a shelf and on a homepage, talk about consent and chemicals in the same breath, and stay credible to a buyer who'd been either ignored or talked down to.

Gender sensitivity ran through every decision. Two failures to avoid: the wink that alienates, and the euphemism that hides.

</SpineSection>

<SpineSection label="Market Thesis" subhead="Women take charge of their sexual health — from home, or from a phone">

The bet was that the primary purchaser wanted a brand on her side, products she could check, and a way to buy without standing in the aisle. Direct-to-consumer was the access strategy, not just the channel.

</SpineSection>

<SpineSection label="The Turn" subhead="The client came back for femcare">

[Timeline needed: when the first engagement ended, and when Sustain returned.]

After the sexual wellness launch, Sustain came back to extend into organic tampons, pads, and liners. That shifted the business from one-off purchases to a recurring need — and the product, the brand, and the business model had to move together.

The answer was the first build-your-own period kit subscription: a product decision, a pricing model, and a brand promise in one flow.

</SpineSection>
