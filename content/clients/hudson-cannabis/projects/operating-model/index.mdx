Hudson Cannabis was on shelf at New York's first legal dispensary the day it opened, in December 2022. Wholesale orders started arriving in the new year, and that's where the role changed.

Once product was moving, the coordination surface grew fast. Every cycle meant new strains, materials, and formulas; new packaging and lot-specific compliance stickers; wholesale menu updates with pricing and product information; in-store assets and visual merchandising; launch campaigns across digital and social. Any one of those slipping put the others out of sync.

Nobody was holding all of it at once, so I built the system that did — and ended up owning the calls it produced: which products, strains, and blends the raw material went into, what they were called, and when they launched.

The system ran at three speeds. The annual roadmap set direction. The quarterly plan set capacity. The weekly loop set the menu. Leadership surfaced the openings — investor relationships, brand partnerships, access to new equipment and capital — and I built the roadmap against them.

**The filters.** Every release cleared three tests at once. Was there a gap in the competitive landscape? Could the farm make it at quality and volume? Did it extend the brand story rather than dilute it? New categories launched small — enough product to test appetite while the team learned the workflow. We committed and scaled only when demand and internal capacity both proved out.

**The weekly loop.** Fridays, Ezra and I would assess available material and pull samples to test over the weekend. Monday we'd decide what would hit the menu that Friday. I'd sprint the rest of the week on strain and product descriptions, positioning, packaging labels, and content — all of it landing on the wholesale menu Friday afternoon, where buyers had learned to check for what was new. Email, social, and web followed the menu.

Naming, copy, label, and campaign moved in a single week because product development, brand, and marketing weren't separate handoffs. Most brands can't run that loop. We could because the constraint — you can only sell what the harvest gave you — was designed into the process instead of fought against.

*The annual roadmap set direction. The quarterly plan set capacity. The weekly loop set the menu.*

**Coordination.** Running that cadence meant the departments had to move together. Alongside the creative work, I spent time with operations building the practices that let cultivation, processing, sales, and marketing plan against each other rather than in sequence.
