<SpineSection label="Context" subhead="A farm entering a market with no rules">

New York legalized adult-use cannabis before the category had a language. No shelf conventions, no consumer expectations, no agreed vocabulary for effect or potency — and a regulatory framework still being written as products went to market.

Hudson Hemp had a decade of regenerative farming behind it and a CBD business that wouldn't transfer.

<SpineSection label="Complication" subhead="The brand we had wasn't the brand the market needed">

I was brought in as an independent creative director to build marketing for Treaty, the company's existing wellness brand.

Four months in, the work made the problem clear. Treaty relied on poetry to suggest impact it couldn't demonstrate — the abstraction was covering for a missing product story.

</SpineSection>

<SpineSection label="The Turn" subhead="The story was twenty miles up the road">

The product story already existed. It just wasn't the one the brand was pointed at: sun-grown cannabis in living soil, on a farm that had spent a decade building regenerative practice. Hemp heals humans the way it heals soil.

That was a real difference, and it was the one thing a New York consumer couldn't get from a national brand.

<SpineSection label="Approach" subhead="Moving to the farm">

I made the case internally for a new brand built on the farm itself. Hudson Cannabis was approved.

Then I moved to the Hudson Valley and set up an office on-site. Brand, product, and marketing decisions were being made in different rooms, and proximity was the only way to work on them as one system rather than inherit them secondhand.

</SpineSection>

<SpineSection label="Market Thesis" subhead="Cannabis as farmers-market produce">

New York City already pays a premium for Hudson Valley produce. Provenance is legible there in a way it isn't in most markets — people know the farms, shop the greenmarkets, and will pay more for tomatoes when they can see where they came from and who grew them.

The bet was that cannabis would follow the same logic. Not a luxury brand, a farm brand. Restraint was the strategy: the product and its origin did the selling.

Flower proved it first — it carried a premium against category average, and buyers paid it. The credibility then transferred. Prerolls, concentrates, and the rest of the line held higher prices than comparable products because the farm had already established what the name meant.

The brand wasn't decorating the product. It was doing the pricing work.

</SpineSection>

<SpineSection label="Entry" subhead="Dimes to seed the state">

The first SKU was a single gram in a clear-front pouch at $10 — a format New York already knew. The dime bag has a long history in the legacy market, and the legal market had mostly abandoned it for opaque mylar and heavy branding. Keeping it clear meant the product sold itself: hand-trimmed, visible, nothing to hide behind. Compliance copy went small on the back.

One object had to read to four different buyers. To the legacy consumer, a familiar format. To the conscious consumer, a degradable film and a farm behind it. To the New York tastemaker, restraint — small type, no color, closer to a fashion label than a cannabis brand. To the farmers-market shopper, visible product and plain naming.

**Material.** Most sustainable packaging shifts the work to the customer: a recyclable label, a bin to find, an effort the brand takes credit for and the buyer performs. Someone leaving a dispensary is looking for relief, not a second errand. We used a newer degradable film that met child-resistance and New York's packaging requirements, at a higher per-unit cost — absorbing the expense rather than advertising a virtue the customer had to complete. No green claims on pack.

**Distribution.** Ten dollars is a low-risk yes for a customer who doesn't know strains yet, or is shopping to a budget, or has never heard of us — and an easy upsell for a budtender who has. The Dimes were on shelf at Housing Works the day it opened in December 2022 — the first legal adult-use dispensary in New York State. From there the format put Hudson Cannabis in dispensaries across the state at volume, and the premium products followed the reputation it built.

## Results

- Around $500K/month in sales
- #1 best-selling hash in New York (Headset)
- 200–250 dispensaries statewide (2025)
- ~1,000 budtenders hosted at the farm
- 20,000 organic Instagram followers
- Four products first to market in New York
- Acquired, December 2025
